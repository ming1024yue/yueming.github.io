In 1984, Warren Buffett published an essay titled *The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville*[^1] . He opened with a thought experiment: imagine 225 million Americans call the flip of a coin every morning. If they call correctly, they win a dollar from those who called wrong. Each day the losers drop out and winners continue. After 20 days, there would be about 215 people who have correctly called coin flips 20 times in a row and each have turned one dollar into over $ 1 million.

Some of those 215 people will probably write books on "How I turned a Dollar into a Million in Twenty days" and share their "secret" on becoming rich in a strategic approach.

It seems that getting rich is pure luck.

In the real world, many factors can make someone rich. Luck, undeniably, is one of the most important.However, Running a business is more complex than a coin toss. I did research on several of the world's leading investors to find out what they have in common besides luck.

I distilled six key principles from studying these investors.

## The First Rule of Fishing: Fish Where the Fish Are

Investing is just like Fishing. As a long term fisherman, the challenge is to find a pond with a high ratio of fish to fishermen, where fish are plentiful and few fishermen are competing for them. There are certain countries or industries just draws a lot of attention, while some do not. In a more competitive environment, the probability to hunt a lot of fish is low.  Investors like Li Lu and Charlie Munger don’t limit themselves to one country, they actively search for opportunities in markets across the United States, Asia, and Europe. In 2008, Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger bought shares in BYD, a Chinese battery maker, for roughly $1 per share, and finally give them 30 times return after 17 years holding. The reason Charlie invested in BYD was his confidence in the company's leading battery technology and the urgent need for clean energy as China’s economy grew rapidly. 

Look for the most dynamic economies or industries, especially in places with well-developed financial systems.
##  Margin of Safety  as the Central Concept of Investment

Market is unpredictable, so it is important to keep safe in it. Popularized by Benjamin Graham, the margin of safety is the gap between a company’s estimated intrinsic value and its current market price. It is often expressed as the percentage by which the market price is below intrinsic value. Suppose an investor estimates a company’s intrinsic value at $100 per share. If the stock trades at $70, the 30% discount represents a margin of safety. The margin of safety cannot prevent investor from loss, but it can protect investors when market is fear or company's future income is decline. That is why those top investors always look for undervalued company. There are many examples can illustrate this key principle, I will give one of them.

In a 2006 talk at Columbia Business School, Seth Klarman[^2] said that Baupost(his investment company) had bought Enron debt for roughly 10 to 15 cents on the dollar. His team spent years analyzing the company’s complex assets and liabilities, and estimated that creditors might recover substantially more than the purchase price.

This illustrates how panic and forced selling can push prices below a security’s potential recovery value. Even under a pessimistic scenario, Klarman believed the debt could be worth more than Baupost paid. 

Investors have comfort by buying at a discount when things can go wrong, for example, bad luck, misjudgment, and market crash.

Warren Buffett once made a vivid analogy for the margin of safety: when you build a bridge with bearing capacity 30,000 lb, you won't drive a 30,000 pounds trucks, you would drive only 10,000 pounds trucks.

## Stay in the Circle of Competence

"If we have a strength, it is in recognizing when we are operating well within our circle of competence and when we are approaching the perimeter."[^3] This is the word that Warren Buffett wrote in 1999 to the shareholders of Berkshire Hathaway. Berkshire is known for its conservative investment approach, and Buffett has traditionally been cautious about investing in technology companies. Because fast-moving technology companies fall outsite his circle of competence, he can not predict the future of such rapid-evolving businesses.

To stay in the circle of competence, the don't do list of Berkshire are: [^4]
+ Avoid business whose futures they cannot evaluate, no matter how exciting their products may be.
+ Never dependent on the kindness of strangers.
+ Let subsidiaries operate on their own, without supervising and monitoring to any degree.
+ Make no attempt to woo Wall Street.

The hardest part of staying in the circle of competence, in my opinion, is truly honest with oneself. Confucius gave the answer more than 2000 years ago:" Knowing what you know, and knowing what you do not know, is true wisdom."

Intellectual honesty means recognizing the limits of what we know. It requires us to test our claims against objective reality—in other words, to make them falsifiable. A claim becomes meaningful when we can clearly state what evidence would show it to be wrong. An unfalsifiable claim is not a reliable one. People often make claims that cannot be falsified, usually out of vanity or because they have not thought them through carefully.

I think it’s useful to ask ourselves three questions whenever we make a judgment or express an opinion:

1. What exactly is my view?
    
2. What assumptions does it depend on?
    
3. Under what circumstances would it be wrong?

The real risk is not knowing what you are doing.
## A Share of Stock Represents Partial Ownership in a Company

A stock is more than a piece of paper or a code to trade, it represents a part of a company. This gives investors a psychological advantage by helping them take a long-term view of a business.

A short quiz: if you plan to eat hamburgers throughout your life and are not a cattle producer, should you wish for higher or lower price for beef? [^5] The question answer itself.

While in the stock market, people often sell out their shares when prices fall. For a net buyer of a company, like people who regularly eat hamburgers, should welcome lower prices, because they can buy more shares for the same amount of money. But there are premises for this case: first, that the company’s fundamentals remain sound, and second, that its management team is honest and competent.

The mindset of owning a part of companies also helps investors focus on businesses themselves rather than on market movements or other people’s opinions, which is, in my view, what distinguishes investors from speculators.  Long-term investors only focus on the fundamentals of businesses, like business model and (future) cash flow; Speculators, by contrast, buy and sell securities based on the prediction of other people's (or market's) behavior.


## Competition Is For Losers

"Competition is for losers" - This contrarian idea comes from venture capitalist Peter Thiel, who co-founded PayPal and Palantir, made early investment in Facebook, SpaceX and OpenAI.

We always think that the losers are the people who cannot compete effectively enough. In conventional thinking, losers are the people whose grades or test scores aren't good; losers are the people who is the slowest runner in track team. This reinforces the assumption that competition is inherently good.

Investing by its very nature is contrarian. A great company or business model contains the genes of a monopoly.  Economics textbooks teach us that perfect competition drives profits to zero. Each great company is success in its own way; All bad companies are alike, which is just contradict with Tolstoy‘s "All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way."  Monopoly, often described as moat by Warren Buffett, is the business that is difficult for competitors to replicate simply with capital.  For example, the market capital for Coca-Cola in 2026 Q4 is around $ 411 billion, if a competitor with such money (or even more) what to build a beverage company to compete with Coca-Cola, he probably will fail. That is the power of a moat: it rests on more than capital. It can also means a strong brand, organizational culture, operational capabilities, and other advantages that are hard to replicate.

In the real world, it is often hard to distinguish monopolies from competitive businesses. One reason is that monopolies tend to downplay their dominance, while companies in highly competitive markets often pretend they are monopolies to attract investors. But there are certain traits of a "monopoly" company. First, they usually have high gross margin over a long term. Second, they can raise prices without significantly reducing demand or losing many customers. Third, they generate ample and consistent cash flow.

As Peter Lynch says, "Competition may prove hazardous to human wealth."
## Simplicity Is the Ultimate Sophistication

I think intellect can be understood in four levels: Smart, Intelligent, Genius, Simple. The highest level of intelligent is simplicity. Thinking about all great physicists in human history, including Newton, Maxwell and Einstein, they sought simple, elegant equations and ideas to describe the universe.

Simplicity implies concentration. Gaining a deep understanding of a business often takes time. For example, one Seth Klarman's analyst spent four years analyzing Enron’s assets and liabilities as the firm evaluated the company’s distressed debt. Charlie Munger believed that an investor could build lasting wealth by holding a concentrated portfolio of a few excellent companies for the long term. Warren Buffett has invested in hundreds of securities, but only a handful of those investments have generated a substantial share of his wealth.

Simplicity also means saying No. Steve Jobs said," Focusing is about saying no, Innovation is saying no to 1,000 things."  Warren Buffett has repeatedly emphasized that patience is essential to successful investing. You don’t get paid for activity, you only get paid for being right.

## Beyond Rich

"If all you succeed in doing life is getting rich by buying little pieces of paper, it's a failed life. Life is more than being shrewd in wealth accumulation" by Charlie Munger.

What I’ve learned from these brilliant investors is that we should think independently, even when it means going against the crowd; remain patient and focus; and conduct thorough deep research. Above all, making decisions based on reason rather than emotion. In investing. And in life.
